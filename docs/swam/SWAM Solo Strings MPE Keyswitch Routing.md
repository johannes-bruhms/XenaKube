# **Analysis of MIDI Polyphonic Expression and Keyswitch Routing in Physical Modeling Synthesis: A Case Study of SWAM Solo Strings**

## **The Paradigm Shift in Virtual Instrument Architecture**

The evolution of digital orchestration and virtual instrument design has historically been anchored by pulse-code modulation (PCM) sampling. This traditional paradigm relies on the meticulous recording of instrumentalists executing specific, isolated articulations—such as short staccato bursts, sustained vibrato notes, or aggressive pizzicato plucks—which are subsequently mapped across a digital keyboard and triggered via standard Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) events.1 While this methodology is highly effective for capturing the static, momentary acoustic fingerprint of an instrument, it introduces severe limitations when attempting to emulate the dynamic, continuous, and highly expressive nature of acoustic performances, particularly those of bowed string instruments.1 In standard sample libraries, transitioning from one articulation to another necessitates loading and triggering discrete audio files, often resulting in discernible phasing artifacts or unnatural crossfades when continuous energy input is required.2

To circumvent the inherent rigidities of sample playback, alternative synthesis paradigms have been developed, most notably Synchronous Wave Acoustic Modeling (SWAM). Conceived by engineers Stefano Lucato and Emanuele Parravicini at Audio Modeling, the SWAM engine eschews traditional sample pools in favor of complex algorithmic frameworks that dynamically model the physical properties, acoustic resonance, and behavioral physics of an instrument in real time.4 This multi-vector, phase-synchronous technology operates more akin to a highly sophisticated synthesizer than a playback sampler.2 The engine computes the interaction of acoustic variables—such as string tension, bow friction, structural resonance, and fingerboard interference—allowing for continuous energy input and multidimensional control.2 Consequently, the software footprint of these physically modeled instruments is exceptionally lightweight, often requiring fewer than fifty megabytes of storage space compared to the tens of gigabytes demanded by high-end orchestral sample libraries.5

The SWAM Solo Strings suite—comprising highly detailed models of the Violin, Viola, Cello, and Double Bass—requires the digital orchestrator to actively "play" the instrument using continuous MIDI controllers, breath controllers, or advanced multidimensional surfaces.4 The mathematical engine computes the transitions between acoustic states ranging from sharp martelé and spiccato to fluid legato and portamento in real time, eliminating the mechanical transitions that plague traditional architectures.9 However, the shift from triggering discrete audio files to continuously modulating a mathematical model introduces profound complexities regarding how control data is parsed and routed. This complexity is magnified exponentially when interfacing the SWAM engine with modern, high-bandwidth control protocols such as MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE).

The central inquiry of this analysis addresses a critical intersection of these technologies: Within an MPE environment, where note data is distributed across independent channels, does the SWAM Solo Strings engine route keyswitch commands on a per-channel basis? Determining whether a composer can simultaneously trigger discrete, independent articulations on a per-voice basis within a single instance of the software requires an exhaustive analysis of the MPE protocol, the SWAM engine's routing topology, and the fundamental constraints of physical modeling.

## **The Architecture of MIDI Polyphonic Expression**

To fully comprehend the mechanisms of keyswitch routing within a multidimensional virtual instrument, one must first delineate the structural topology of MIDI Polyphonic Expression. The original MIDI 1.0 specification, adopted in the early 1980s, operates on a strictly channel-wide basis. Parameters such as Pitch Bend, Channel Pressure (Aftertouch), and Control Change (CC) messages apply universally and indiscriminately to all notes currently active on a specific MIDI channel.11 If a performer executes a chord and engages the physical pitch bend wheel, the entire chord shifts in pitch simultaneously. This limitation fundamentally precludes the emulation of advanced acoustic techniques, such as applying a microtonal vibrato to a sustained melody note while holding a static drone note on a lower string.6

MIDI Polyphonic Expression fundamentally subverts this systemic limitation by distributing individual notes across multiple, discrete MIDI channels, appropriately termed "Member Channels." In a standard MPE configuration, Channel 1 or Channel 16 is designated as the "Master Channel," functioning to handle global parameter changes, while the remaining channels (e.g., Channels 2 through 15\) are dynamically allocated to host individual note events.3 When a performer strikes a key on an MPE-compatible hardware controller—such as the ROLI Seaboard, Expressive E Osmose, or Roger Linn Design LinnStrument—the device assigns that specific note to its own distinct member channel.12

This channel allocation allows the performer to execute a continuous pitch bend on one note, apply heavy, pressure-sensitive aftertouch on a second note, and modulate the timbral brightness (typically via CC74) on a third note, all concurrently and with absolute expressive independence.14 The integration of MPE hardware is natively supported within the SWAM Solo Strings software environment.12 The software's mathematical engine parses these incoming member channels in real time, mapping the discrete control vectors to specific physical behavioral parameters, such as modulating the virtual string tension based on discrete pitch bend data, or mapping bow pressure derived independently from per-channel pressure messages.3

Despite this vast capability for independent, per-voice modulation of continuous expression parameters, the physical reality of a solo string instrument imposes strict, hardcoded constraints on how the concept of polyphony is conceptualized within the algorithm. These physical boundaries directly dictate how the engine processes discrete channel data, particularly concerning macro-level state changes invoked by keyswitches.

## **Parsing the Core Query: Keyswitch Routing in MPE Frameworks**

The definitive inquiry driving this technical report is whether SWAM Solo Strings, when operating in its MPE Profile mode, routes keyswitches per channel, thereby permitting independent articulations for simultaneously sounding notes within a single software instance. By analyzing the routing parameters, interface architecture, and physical modeling logic of the SWAM engine, the evidence yields a conclusive reality: **Keyswitches in SWAM Solo Strings are not routed per channel to enable per-voice articulation independence; instead, they serve exclusively as global state modifiers for the entire physical model instance.**

### **MIDI Channel Routing and MPE Forcing Mechanisms**

The configuration interface of the SWAM engine features highly specific routing parameters designed to handle incoming MIDI streams. The primary parameter, Receive MIDI Channel, dictates the core channel over which the sound generator receives note data and the continuous controllers that directly affect the physical model's output.3 In a standard Legacy MIDI 1.0 profile, this parameter can be assigned to any singular, discrete channel from 1 to 16, or switched to ANY to enable Omni mode reception.3 However, the SWAM documentation explicitly mandates that when the software is switched to the MPE profile, the Receive MIDI Channel is forcibly locked to ANY.3 Because an MPE controller dynamically distributes notes across Channels 2 through 15 in real time, the SWAM sound generation engine must remain perpetually open to receiving note and continuous expression data across the entire channel spectrum to process the polyphonic expression vectors accurately.

Distinct from the primary note reception channel is the KS MIDI Channel (Keyswitches MIDI Channel) parameter. This separate control allows the user to isolate keyswitch reception to a specific MIDI channel (1-16) or set it to ANY.3 The technical manuals clarify that the KS MIDI Channel can intentionally differ from the Receive MIDI Channel.3 This architectural decision provides immense flexibility, allowing a performer to utilize an external, secondary MIDI controller—such as a dedicated pedalboard or a secondary keyboard transmitting on a fixed, distinct channel—exclusively for triggering keyswitch articulations, while their primary MPE controller handles note generation and micro-expression on the dynamically allocated member channels.3

If the KS MIDI Channel is configured to ANY, a keyswitch command—for instance, a C-1 Note On message meant to trigger the *Bow* Play Mode—transmitted over any MPE member channel (e.g., Channel 3\) will be successfully received, recognized, and parsed by the SWAM engine.17 However, the successful reception and parsing of the MIDI message on a distinct channel must not be conflated with the maintenance of independent, per-channel articulation states.

### **The Distinction Between Global State and Per-Voice Independence**

When a keyswitch event is received on any channel by the SWAM engine, the software updates the physical model's global articulation state.18 This behavior is rooted in the fundamental definition of physical modeling. The SWAM Solo Strings engine is a mathematical representation of a *single* acoustic body—a solitary violin, viola, cello, or double bass.19 This stands in stark contrast to multitimbral sample playback engines, which can effortlessly trigger a recorded pizzicato audio sample on voice one and a recorded arco audio sample on voice two within the exact same plugin instance.9 The SWAM engine does not play audio files; it operates by dynamically restructuring its internal synthesis equations based on the selected Play Mode.9

Within the SWAM architecture, the primary keyswitch states represent mutually exclusive physical and mathematical paradigms 18:

| Primary Play Mode | Keyswitch Default | Physical Model Behavior |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Bow** | C | The instrument is mathematically simulated as being continuously excited by the friction of a horsehair bow drawn across the strings, generating continuous spectral energy. |
| **Pizzicato** | C (Double Tap) | The instrument is simulated as being impulsively excited by fingers plucking the strings, requiring entirely different attack, decay, and fingerboard interference calculations. |
| **Col Legno** | C (Triple Tap) | The strings are struck with the wooden stick of the bow, producing a percussive, brief excitation with unique harmonic resonance characteristics. |

Because these parameters govern the fundamental physical behavior of the single modeled acoustic instrument, the Play Mode operates as an absolute, global parameter.18 While advanced, real-world string techniques occasionally feature left-hand pizzicato paired with right-hand bowing 23, the SWAM algorithmic architecture simplifies the macro-state of the virtual instrument into these primary, exclusive modes for the sake of computational stability and control.18

Consequently, if a performer holds a sustained note on MPE Channel 2 (playing in the *arco* bowed mode) and subsequently triggers the Pizzicato keyswitch using MPE Channel 3, the entire instance of the SWAM physical model immediately shifts to the Pizzicato simulation algorithm. The engine does not isolate the new keyswitch state exclusively to Channel 3; it globally reframes the physical model for all active and subsequent notes. This structural reality confirms that keyswitch routing in SWAM alters the macro-environment of the instrument globally, strictly precluding independent per-voice articulation within a single software instance.

## **Polyphonic Emulation and String Crossing Algorithms**

The assertion that keyswitches strictly govern the global state of the instrument demands a deeper examination of how the SWAM engine manages polyphony. While the physical model is heavily rooted in the simulation of monophonic melodic lines 19, string repertoire frequently necessitates the performance of double stops (bichords). The SWAM Solo Strings suite incorporates sophisticated algorithms to detect and mathematically process these simultaneous notes.18

### **The "Auto" Polyphony Paradigm and Look-Ahead Latency**

Within the SWAM user interface, the Polyphony parameter governs the instrument's capacity to handle multiple simultaneous notes. When this parameter is set to the "Auto" (Double/Mono) mode, the engine's physical model permits the performance of two simultaneous initial notes, effectively creating a double stop.24 To execute this realistically without predictive knowledge of the performer's intent, the engine must actively differentiate between a rapid legato trill (which should be processed as a monophonic transition) and a true bichord.24

To achieve this differentiation, the software introduces a calculated look-ahead delay during the attack phase of a note.24 This delay is governed by the Auto Poly Detect. Time parameter, which specifies the precise temporal threshold required for the algorithmic detection of a double stop.4 If a second note is received within this millisecond window, the engine processes the event as a bichord; if not, it proceeds as a monophonic melodic line. Because this extra processing latency is inherently appended to the attack of staccato notes when the polyphony mode is set to "Auto," professional users relying on highly responsive MPE controllers are advised to minimize this value to preserve tight rhythmic accuracy during rapid passages.4

If the engine successfully detects two simultaneous notes, it mathematically generates the bichord within the physical model. Remarkably, fluid legato and continuous portamento transitions remain possible for these paired notes simultaneously.24 However, both notes generated within this bichord exist completely bound to the same global physical model state. If the global Play Mode keyswitch is currently set to "Bow", both virtual strings are bowed. If the global mode is switched to "Pizzicato", the engine seamlessly transitions both notes to utilize the "Pizzicato Poly" algorithmic model.24 At no point does the physical model permit a divergence of the fundamental physical articulation between the two sounding voices, unequivocally validating the principle that MPE channel data cannot isolate keyswitch states.

### **Mono String Crossing Mechanics and Interval Tracking**

In scenarios where true polyphonic double stops are not desired, the SWAM engine provides advanced emulations for overlapping string transitions. The Mono String Crossing parameter actively influences the acoustic behavior of overlapping notes spanning adjacent strings when the core polyphony parameter is restricted to the "Mono SX" mode.18 In this specialized mode, if the musical interval performed between a currently held note and a newly triggered note falls within a specific, user-defined semitone range, the engine artificially dampens and stops the release phase of the "old" string.18 The available interval tracking options include:

| Interval Setting | Semitone Range | Musical Equivalents |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **1 sem** | 1 semitone | Minor 2nd |
| **2 sem** | 1 or 2 semitones | Minor 2nd, Major 2nd |
| **Harm** | 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11, 13 semitones | Minor/Major 2nd, Minor/Major 3rd, Tritone, Minor/Major 6th, Major 7th, Minor 9th |
| **All** | All intervals | Any note transition |

For intervals that fall outside the selected option parameter, the physical model permits the preceding virtual string to vibrate freely during the transition, generating a momentary, authentic overlap of two tones that accurately simulates the acoustic bleed of real adjacent strings.18 Even in this highly nuanced state of brief string overlap, both vibrating tones are fundamentally subject to the exact same macro-articulation state governed by the most recently received global keyswitch.

## **The Typology and Configuration of SWAM Keyswitches**

While the keyswitches in the SWAM engine operate strictly on a global level, the mechanisms by which they are configured, mapped, and parsed by the software are exceptionally granular. Audio Modeling has engineered these configurations to accommodate a diverse array of hardware controllers and individual performance methodologies.

### **Velocity Mapping and Trigger Modes**

In traditional, sample-based virtual instruments, keyswitches are entirely binary: depressing the MIDI key C0 instantly activates one articulation, while depressing D0 activates another. Audio Modeling has pioneered a more responsive and space-efficient paradigm to minimize the physical keyboard real estate required for articulation management. The K.S. Mode (trigg/vel) parameter dictates exactly how the mathematical engine interprets incoming keyswitch notes.18

The first option is the **Trigg (Trigger) Mode**. In this configuration, a specific keyswitch status is advanced sequentially by rapidly triggering the identical physical key multiple times. For example, if the primary Play Mode keyswitch is mapped to the C key, depressing the key once engages the "Bow" model. Double-tapping the C key rapidly shifts the engine to the "Pizzicato" model, and triple-tapping the key engages the "Col Legno" model.18 This methodology is highly advantageous for performers utilizing compact MIDI controllers or MPE surfaces with limited physical key ranges, such as a 25-key travel controller.

The alternative option is the **Vel (Velocity) Mode**. When this mode is active, the articulation status is determined by the specific MIDI Note On velocity with which the designated keyswitch is struck.18 A very light, low-velocity tap might trigger the Arco model, a medium-velocity strike could trigger Pizzicato, while a high-velocity, forceful strike triggers Col Legno. The precise boundaries of these velocity-dependent zones are entirely customizable via the KS Velocity Remap parameter, which empowers the user to control the exact numerical distribution of the velocity thresholds between the discrete states.4

### **Octave Transposition and Keyboard Zoning**

Given the immense diversity of modern MIDI controllers—ranging from 88-key weighted piano beds to specialized MPE surfaces—the physical location of the keyswitches must remain highly flexible. The KS Octave (Key Switches Octave Transpose) parameter permits the orchestrator to shift the entire contiguous block of keyswitch assignments up or down the MIDI spectrum.4 The lowest assignable range begins at C-1, extending upwards to C2.17

If the physical range of the simulated string instrument mathematically overlaps with the designated keyswitch zone, an internal MIDI conflict occurs, potentially resulting in performed notes inadvertently triggering radical articulation changes.25 To prevent this critical error, users can completely disable the keyswitch block by setting the Octave Transpose parameter to "OFF".4 Within the graphical user interface (GUI) of the standalone application or plugin, active keyswitches are distinctively colored red on the virtual keyboard to clearly demarcate the non-playable control zones from the playable instrument range.25

## **Articulation Directives and Physical Modifiers**

Beyond the primary Play Mode, the SWAM Solo Strings v3 architecture maps several highly specific acoustic behaviors to default keyswitches, each defining a critical aspect of the global physical model's output.

### **Bow Direction and Gesture Algorithms**

The physical mechanics of bowing are modeled with immense depth. The Bow Start parameter is conventionally mapped to Key Switch F, granting the performer the ability to force the physical model to initiate the subsequent staccato attack with either an **Up Bow** or a **Down Bow**.17 The gesture mode governing the continuous dynamics of the bow can also be fundamentally manipulated via keyswitching.

When the Gesture Mode is set to "Bowing" (as opposed to standard continuous Expression), the physical input controller—such as an expression pedal, modulation wheel, or the Y-axis of an MPE key—acts as a direct digital analogue for the physical displacement of the bow.17 The speed of the controller's physical movement directly determines the speed of the virtual bow dragging across the virtual string, a calculation that is further modulated by the Bowing Sensitivity parameter.17 This mode offers unparalleled realism but demands high technical proficiency from the user.

Alternatively, a "Bipolar" gesture mode can be engaged, translating the incoming expression data into bidirectional bow speed. In this configuration, a median MIDI CC value of 63 mathematically represents absolute zero speed. CC values dropping below 63 emulate the physics of down-bowing, while values rising above 63 emulate the physics of up-bowing.17

### **Tremolo Mechanics and Auto-Modulation**

The execution of a tremolo is mapped by default to Key Switch G\#, and it acts as a global modulator superseding the standard bowing algorithm.17 The parameter offers "Off", "Slow", and "Fast" physical states. The exact physical oscillation frequency of the tremolo effect is determined algorithmically by the Tremolo Min Speed parameter.18 If the user opts to synchronize the tremolo to the host DAW's tempo, this parameter acts as a base threshold; otherwise, the "Fast" tremolo operates mathematically at exactly 1.5 times the speed of the designated "Slow" setting.17

Furthermore, advanced performers can achieve manual, granular tremolo control using Key Switch C\#. The Manual Bowing KeySwitch parameter dictates the exact function of this key. If the parameter is set to "Tremolo", pressing and subsequently releasing the C\# key forces a rapid, instantaneous bow change upon both the Note On and the Note Off MIDI events, effectively allowing the performer to tap out a custom tremolo rhythm.11 If set to "Bow Change", a direction reversal occurs solely on the Note On event, and the specific velocity of the keyswitch strike mathematically influences the dynamic accent of the simulated bow stroke.11 Notably, if an auto-tremolo is globally engaged via Key Switch G\#, the physical model automatically forces the gesture mode back to standard "Expression," safely overriding complex Bipolar modes, because the automated tremolo algorithm inherently dictates the rapid, mathematically precise alternation of the bow direction.17

### **Bow Lift and String Dampening Physics**

The accurate simulation of string resonance during the post-attack release phase is a critical component of the acoustic model. Key Switch E governs the Bow Lift state.17 This parameter fundamentally alters the release envelope characteristics of the modeled string. When set to "Off String," the algorithm simulates the physical action of lifting the horsehair bow completely away from the string upon the Note Off event, allowing for a natural, rapid attenuation or a specifically tailored release tail. Conversely, when set to "On String," the algorithm simulates the instrumentalist halting the bow's movement while purposefully maintaining physical contact with the string. This action effectively chokes the vibration, causing an abrupt and highly realistic dampening of the acoustic sound.17 While primarily impacting the release envelope, the Bow Lift parameter also slightly alters the friction characteristics of the subsequent attack phase, demonstrating the interconnected nature of the physical modeling algorithms.17

## **Hardware Controller Interfacing and Vector Mapping**

The abstract routing of keyswitches and continuous controllers has profound, direct implications for hardware configuration. Operating a multi-vector physical model demands highly sophisticated control surfaces. SWAM Solo Strings fully supports a broad spectrum of advanced controllers, but the specific hardware chosen significantly alters how parameters like Pitch Bend, Expression, and Keyswitches must be physically routed and configured within the software.

### **Interfacing with MPE Hardware Surfaces**

Hardware devices such as the ROLI Seaboard, Expressive E Osmose, and Roger Linn Design LinnStrument possess distinct physical interfaces that necessitate highly specific SWAM parameter adjustments to ensure accurate data translation.3

A critical variable when interfacing with MPE is the Pitch Bend Curve parameter. When a user employs a traditional, non-MPE MIDI keyboard alongside an external, physically separate controller (such as a standalone modulation wheel or a breath controller), switching the Pitch Bend Curve to "ON" introduces a non-linear response curve.3 This computational curve mathematically compensates for the lack of direct physical correspondence between the keybed playing surface and the external pitch wheel.

Conversely, when utilizing a true MPE device where the pitch manipulation is integrated directly into the multidimensional playing surface—such as sliding a finger laterally across the silicone surface of a Seaboard or the physical keys of a LinnStrument—the Pitch Bend Curve must be strictly set to "OFF".3 Disabling the curve enforces a strict, linear pitch bend that corresponds precisely 1:1 with the physical micro-movements of the performer's finger, enabling highly accurate glissandos and expressive microtonal vibratos.13 For such MPE slides to function seamlessly across the full virtual fingerboard length without introducing audible pitch stepping or digital artifacts, the Max P.B. Range should be set to its maximum algorithmic value, typically 48 semitones.18

### **Wind and Breath Controller Specifics**

While MPE excels at defining polyphonic continuous control, a significant portion of SWAM users deploy alternative multidimensional devices, specifically wind controllers (e.g., Akai EWI, Yamaha WX series, Aodyo Sylphyo) or dedicated breath controllers (such as the TEControl series).12 These controllers are highly expressive, translating lung pressure into MIDI data, but they are fundamentally monophonic devices, presenting their own distinct parameter requirements within the SWAM engine.

The Breath Ctrl Mode can be toggled "ON" to radically modify the engine's physical attack behavior.17 In standard operation, initiating a new note requires a discrete MIDI Note On event from a keyboard. However, in Breath Control mode, if a performer sustains a fingered note on the controller and relies solely on their breath envelope to pulse the volume (simulating techniques like flutter tonguing or rhythmic breath accents), the SWAM engine can dynamically trigger sequential, highly realistic attack algorithms without ever receiving new MIDI Note On messages.17 The Breath Ctrl Hi-Res Threshold parameter allows the user to define the precise micro-level of incoming continuous expression data required to trigger this mathematical new attack, while the Breath Ctrl Attack Sens. parameter dictates the resulting dynamic strength of the simulated bow strike or breath pulse.17

Furthermore, electronic wind controllers inherently transmit Note Off events the exact millisecond the performer ceases blowing and the physical expression value drops to zero. To optimize the physical model's performance—particularly for rapid articulation techniques like double or triple-tonguing—the Wind Controller Release Mode can be engaged within the settings.21 This setting forces the engine's Release Time parameter to its absolute minimum, entirely disabling extended release tails and ensuring the instrument remains highly reactive, rhythmically precise, and immune to overlapping note bleed.26 Lastly, users configuring external hardware like the EWI 4000s are strictly warned against configuring the hardware to mix pitch-bend or vibrato directly into the primary breath sensor MIDI stream. Because the SWAM physical model is designed to interpret these vectors independently to compute physical mass and friction, merging them on the hardware side results in highly unnatural, unpredictable, and mathematically flawed vibrato artifacts.11

## **Circumventing Global Limitations via Multi-Instance Architectures**

Having exhaustively established that SWAM Solo Strings fundamentally processes keyswitches globally across a single instance—even when receiving highly granular MPE data across discrete channels—a critical orchestration question remains: How does a digital composer sequence a string section where individual voices must articulate independently? If the musical requirement dictates a polyphonic *divisi* passage wherein the first chair violin performs a soaring *arco* melody, while the second chair violin performs a rapid, highly rhythmic *pizzicato* backing line, relying on a single plugin instance is a functional impossibility due to the global nature of the physical model.18

To achieve true, per-voice articulation independence, users must transcend the limitations of the single physical model by deploying a multi-instance architecture. This methodology involves hosting multiple, discrete instances of the SWAM plugin on separate tracks within a Digital Audio Workstation (DAW) or a live performance hosting application such as Camelot Pro.4

### **External Routing Topologies and Divisimate Integration**

Advanced external MIDI routing software, most notably Divisimate by Nextmidi, is explicitly designed to solve this exact multitimbral bottleneck. Divisimate functions as an intermediary layer, intercepting incoming MIDI data (whether originating from a standard MIDI 1.0 keyboard or a multidimensional MPE controller) and intelligently distributing the individual voices of a performed chord across multiple discrete virtual MIDI ports.30 Each virtual port is subsequently routed into the DAW to trigger a completely separate, independent instance of the SWAM instrument.

A critical feature of the Divisimate 2 software architecture is its "Global Keyswitch Area".30 Because Divisimate splits a single live performance across multiple plugin instances, attempting to manage the keyswitch state individually for each separate instance could quickly become an overwhelming, unmanageable burden for the performer. The Global Keyswitch Area allows the orchestrator to define a specific, customizable zone on their primary MIDI controller.30 When a keyswitch is physically depressed within this defined zone, Divisimate uniformly and instantaneously transmits that specific articulation change to *all* active instrument ports simultaneously.

Conversely, if completely independent articulations are required to achieve the arco/pizzicato *divisi* split described earlier, the Divisimate routing matrix can be meticulously configured to send unique keyswitch parameters exclusively to highly targeted ports. In this advanced multi-instance topology, the independent member channels of an MPE performance effectively become discrete, standalone physical models. This complex architecture allows the composer to wield absolute, granular autonomy over both the continuous micro-expression (pitch, pressure, timbre) and the macro-articulation states (bowed, plucked) of every individual voice in the ensemble.

## **Spatial Coherence and the Ambiente Environment**

Historically, utilizing multiple instances of a physically modeled string instrument to achieve true independent polyphony introduced severe psychoacoustic flaws. Because each plugin instance independently computed its sound generation without any computational awareness of the others, layering multiple SWAM violins often resulted in destructive phase cancellation and an unnatural, synthetically "stacked" auditory image that lacked spatial depth.31 Furthermore, routing multiple independent, dry mono signals into a master digital reverb bus stripped the ensemble of true acoustic positioning and early reflection accuracy.

To definitively resolve this long-standing issue, Audio Modeling introduced the **Ambiente** Room Simulator, integrated directly into SWAM v3.7+ and the SWAM String Sections ecosystem.32 Ambiente is not a traditional algorithmic insert reverb effect; it is a highly sophisticated, high-level mathematical spatializer. It is explicitly designed to recreate early acoustic reflections based on the precise physical arrangement and spatial geometry of the instruments within a modeled virtual room, providing vastly superior reverberation realism compared to previous iterations of the SWAM engine.33

Crucially, Ambiente features a revolutionary inter-plugin communication protocol. Every discrete instance of a SWAM instrument instantiated within a DAW project natively communicates with all other SWAM instances running in that session.11 They continuously share real-time telemetry regarding their room placement, stereophonic positioning, and acoustic output. Consequently, if a composer utilizes four instances of SWAM Violin to achieve the independent per-channel articulations necessary for a complex *divisi* passage (driven by Divisimate or dedicated MPE tracks), those four discrete instances operate coherently within the exact same virtual acoustic space.11

The user can open the Ambiente interface within any single SWAM plugin instance and holistically manage the placement of all associated instruments without having to tediously open the GUI for each individual track.11 The spatial engine offers 18 unique mathematical combinations of room dimensions and specific absorption materials for precise instrument placement.33 To ensure maximum flexibility and mix integrity during post-production, the Main Volume Processing parameter can be set to "Pre Room"—meaning volume automation affects only the direct sound source and not the algorithmically generated spatial reverberation—or "Post Room," which adjusts the entirely localized output, reverb included.11

Furthermore, when placing multiple identical instruments (e.g., three virtual Violins) in close stereophonic proximity, the Divisi Anti-Phasing parameter must be assigned uniquely to each instance. This critical parameter slightly decouples their complex acoustic calculations, intentionally introducing microscopic variations to prevent the destructive phase interference that plagues physically modeled ensembles.11 Through the masterful integration of MPE control, multi-instance MIDI routing, and the Ambiente spatial engine, true multi-articulation polyphony can be achieved with unparalleled acoustic realism.

### **The Monophonic Constraint of Virtual Adaptive Resizing**

In the context of multi-instance routing and ensemble building, it is highly pertinent to examine how Audio Modeling handles pre-built sections. While SWAM Solo Strings supports the aforementioned Auto-Polyphony for executing double stops, other implementations of the engine, specifically the SWAM String Sections, explicitly forbid polyphony entirely. Within the String Sections module, the core String Model parameter can be set to "Virtual Adaptive Resizing (Mono)".21

This specific physical model does not mathematically represent a traditional four-string instrument. Rather, it represents a theoretical, continuous single string whose physical thickness and structural tension dynamically adapt and resize in real time based on the exact pitch being performed.21 Because this conceptual model features only one physical string at any given millisecond, polyphony is structurally and mathematically impossible. Accordingly, all polyphony-related parameters in the user interface are strictly disabled when this model is active.11 This architecture reinforces the core foundational principle of physical modeling: the limitations of polyphony, string crossing, and articulation splitting are ultimately bound by the mathematical reality of the physical object being synthesized. To achieve polyphony or articulation divergence, the user must simulate multiple physical objects via multiple instances.

## **Workflow Implications for Orchestral Sequencing**

Understanding the strict global nature of keyswitch routing within the SWAM engine carries significant implications for the modern digital orchestrator. When relying on sample libraries, composers often build dense, multitimbral "mega-templates" where a single MIDI track can house a multitude of articulations seamlessly switched via a single keyboard controller.7 The orchestrator can play a complex chord and rely on the sampler to process differing velocities or channel data into a cohesive, mixed-articulation output.

Transitioning to physical modeling requires a fundamental paradigm shift in workflow. Because the SWAM model is a continuous, globally constrained acoustic simulation, the orchestrator must treat the virtual instrument exactly as they would a physical session player.35 A single violinist cannot instantly clone themselves to play a plucked bassline underneath their own bowed melody. Therefore, the digital workflow must be decentralized.

Composers must commit to multi-track sequencing, dedicating specific MIDI tracks (and corresponding SWAM instances) to specific physical roles within the arrangement. If a piece requires alternating rapidly between a bowed section and a plucked section, it is often more computationally efficient and musically coherent to utilize two separate instances of the plugin rather than attempting to sequence rapid, global keyswitch changes within a single MPE stream.31 The integration of tools like Divisimate and the Ambiente Room Simulator bridges this workflow gap, allowing the orchestrator to perform live across multiple physical models while retaining the spatial cohesion of a singular ensemble.30 Ultimately, maximizing the realism of the SWAM engine requires embracing its constraints; by understanding that keyswitches modify the entirety of the simulated acoustic body, the composer can craft sequences that respect the physics of the instrument, resulting in unparalleled expressive authenticity.

## **Conclusion**

The SWAM Solo Strings v3 architecture represents a definitive pinnacle in the real-time physical modeling of acoustic instruments. By moving away from static PCM sample playback, the engine relies heavily on multidimensional continuous control to synthesize highly authentic, phase-synchronous performances. The integration of MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) drastically enhances this expressivity by distributing notes across discrete member channels, allowing for highly individualized, per-note manipulation of critical acoustic vectors such as pitch bend, string tension, and bow pressure.

However, detailed technical analysis of the engine's routing topology, parameter configuration matrix, and underlying physical modeling paradigms reveals a firm architectural boundary regarding keyswitch implementation. While the SWAM engine can flawlessly receive keyswitch events over any MPE member channel (provided the KS MIDI Channel parameter is set to ANY), it does not route or apply these keyswitches on an independent, per-voice, per-channel basis. The underlying mathematical physical model represents a solitary acoustic instrument; therefore, primary articulation modes—such as transitioning from *Bow* to *Pizzicato* or enabling *Col Legno*—operate strictly as global state modifiers. An MPE command dictating a switch to the *Pizzicato* physical model forces the entire simulation into the plucked state, immediately overriding the articulation of any simultaneously sustained notes or double stops.

To transcend this structural limitation and achieve true per-channel articulation independence, composers and performers cannot rely on the internal routing of a single plugin instance. Instead, they must deploy a multi-instance software architecture, leveraging external MIDI routing utilities like Divisimate to distribute discrete voices to separate, dedicated instances of the SWAM instrument. To counteract the phasing and spatial disjointedness historically associated with this multi-track method, the Ambiente Room Simulator provides an essential inter-plugin communication protocol that acoustically unifies disparate SWAM instances into a singular, cohesive spatial environment. Ultimately, while the MPE protocol ensures continuous expressive independence for the virtual performer's individual fingers, the macro-state of the synthesized instrument remains bound by a unified, global physical reality.
